The problem

What is coronary artery disease?

The coronary arteries feed the heart muscle with oxygen-rich blood. Over years, fatty deposits called plaque build up inside them. The channel narrows and less blood gets through, which can cause chest pain or a heart attack.

1

A healthy artery is smooth and wide open.

2

Cholesterol, smoking, diabetes and high blood pressure help plaque form in the wall.

3

As plaque grows, the opening narrows and blood flow slows, especially during exercise.

Clinical detail

Atherosclerosis: LDL enters the intima, is oxidised and taken up by macrophages (foam cells), forming a lipid core under a fibrous cap. Flow-limiting stenosis is usually ≥70% diameter (≥50% for left main). Through the narrowing blood actually speeds up (a jet), but total flow downstream falls when demand rises, causing ischaemia and angina. Rupture of a thin-cap plaque with thrombus causes acute coronary syndromes.

Types of plaque

Not all plaque is the same. What it is made of changes how risky it is and how doctors treat it. Pick a type to see it from the side, in cross-section, and the way an IVUS ultrasound probe inside the artery shows it.

Blood flow 80%

Fibrous (fibrotic) plaque

Usually stable

Made mostly of tough, scar-like tissue. It narrows the artery slowly and is less likely to crack, but if it narrows a lot it causes chest pain on effort.

Treatment
Medicines first; if it limits blood flow, a balloon and stent usually open it well.
On imaging
IVUS shows bright, even tissue with no shadow behind it.
Clinical detail

Pathological intimal thickening / fibrous plaque: smooth-muscle cells in a collagen- and proteoglycan-rich matrix with little lipid. IVUS: homogeneous, iso- to hyperechoic (as bright as or brighter than the adventitia) without acoustic shadowing. OCT: signal-rich, homogeneous, low attenuation. Typical of stable angina; predictable balloon predilatation and stent expansion.

Lipid-rich (soft) plaque

Vulnerable

A soft, fatty core under a thin lid (cap). It may not narrow the artery much, but the thin cap can crack and set off a sudden clot: a heart attack.

Treatment
High-dose statins, stopping smoking and controlling sugar and blood pressure make it more stable. A stent is used if it narrows the artery or has caused a heart attack.
On imaging
IVUS shows a darker, soft-looking area; the tissue behind it can fade away.
Clinical detail

Fibroatheroma with a necrotic core; thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA: cap <65 µm, macrophage-rich, large necrotic core) is the precursor of rupture. IVUS: hypoechoic (echolucent) plaque, often with deep ultrasound attenuation without calcium ("attenuated plaque"); the thin cap is below IVUS resolution, so OCT is needed to measure it, and NIRS gives the lipid-core burden index. Higher risk of distal embolisation and no-reflow during PCI. Intensive lipid lowering (statin ± ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitor) thickens the cap and shrinks the core.

Calcified plaque

Hard

Calcium deposits make the plaque as hard as bone. It is common with age, diabetes and kidney disease. Hard plaque can stop a balloon or stent from opening fully.

Treatment
The calcium is cracked first, with a high-pressure or cutting balloon, a shock-wave balloon (lithotripsy) or a tiny diamond drill (rotablation), then a stent is placed.
On imaging
IVUS shows a very bright arc that casts a black shadow behind it.
Clinical detail

Fibrocalcific plaque (sheet calcium); a calcified nodule is an eruptive form that can cause thrombosis. IVUS: bright (hyperechoic) leading edge with acoustic shadowing and reverberations, so calcium thickness cannot be measured. The IVUS calcium score (superficial arc >270° over >5 mm, 360° calcium, calcified nodule, vessel diameter <3.5 mm) and the OCT score (arc >180°, thickness >0.5 mm, length >5 mm) predict stent under-expansion. Lesion preparation: non-compliant or super-high-pressure balloons, cutting/scoring balloons, intravascular lithotripsy, rotational or orbital atherectomy; confirm expansion with imaging.

Thrombotic plaque (cracked, with a clot)

Emergency

When a plaque cracks, the body treats it like a wound and builds a blood clot on it. The clot can block the artery within minutes: this is a heart attack.

Treatment
Emergency angioplasty. The clot may be sucked out (thrombus aspiration), then a stent is placed. Blood-thinning medicines stop new clots forming.
On imaging
IVUS shows a lumpy, speckled grey mass inside the channel.

See how a clot is removed ↓

Clinical detail

Coronary thrombosis arises from plaque rupture (about 55–65%), plaque erosion (about 25–35%: intact cap, often younger patients, smokers and women) or a calcified nodule (about 5%). On IVUS, thrombus is an intraluminal, often lobulated mass with a speckled ("scintillating") texture and sometimes microchannels; it can be hard to tell from soft plaque. A rupture shows as an echolucent cavity with a torn cap. OCT separates red (RBC- and fibrin-rich, shadowing) from white (platelet-rich) thrombus better. Graded by TIMI thrombus grade. Management: antithrombotic therapy and primary PCI, with selective thrombus aspiration.

Diagnosis · about 30–60 minutes

Coronary angiogram, step by step

An angiogram is a special X-ray movie of the heart's arteries. It shows exactly where, and how badly, an artery is narrowed. Scroll through the steps or press play.

  1. 1Step

    Preparation

    You lie on a padded table in the cath lab. A nurse puts a small drip (IV line) in your arm and sticky ECG pads on your chest to watch your heartbeat.

    About 15–20 min
    Clinical detail

    Confirm consent, fasting per local protocol, allergy history (contrast), renal function (eGFR), anticoagulants (hold warfarin/DOAC per protocol) and metformin plan. Assess the radial artery (Barbeau test or ultrasound). Continuous ECG, NIBP and SpO₂; optional light sedation (e.g., midazolam, fentanyl).

  2. 2Step

    Numbing the wrist or groin

    The doctor numbs a small spot on your wrist (radial artery) or groin (femoral artery) with an injection. It stings for a few seconds, then the area goes numb. You stay awake.

    About 2 min
    Clinical detail

    1–2% lidocaine: ~1–2 mL subcutaneously for radial, 10–15 mL for femoral. Radial access is preferred where expertise allows: fewer bleeding complications and earlier mobilisation (RIVAL, MATRIX). Femoral puncture is made over the femoral head, below the inguinal ligament, ideally ultrasound/fluoroscopy guided.

  3. 3Step

    Placing the sheath

    Through a tiny needle prick, a short soft tube called a sheath is slipped into the artery. It works like a doorway, so the catheters can go in and out safely.

    About 2–3 min
    Clinical detail

    Modified Seldinger technique: needle → 0.018–0.025″ wire → 5–6 Fr hydrophilic sheath. Radial "cocktail" through the side-arm: unfractionated heparin 2,500–5,000 IU (or ~50 IU/kg) to prevent radial occlusion, plus a vasodilator (verapamil 2.5 mg and/or nitroglycerin) against spasm.

  4. 4Step

    Guiding the catheter to the heart

    A thin, flexible tube called a catheter is gently passed through the artery up to the heart, watched on X-ray. Its tip is placed at the opening of a heart artery. You do not feel it travelling inside.

    About 3–5 min
    Clinical detail

    Over a 0.035″ J-wire a 5–6 Fr diagnostic catheter (e.g., Judkins Left 3.5, or a universal Tiger shape from the radial) is advanced. Right radial route: radial → brachial → subclavian → brachiocephalic → ascending aorta. Femoral route: iliac → abdominal and thoracic aorta → arch. Engage the left main ostium and watch the pressure trace for damping or ventricularisation.

  5. 5Step

    Contrast dye lights up the arteries

    A special dye (contrast) is injected through the catheter. It fills the heart arteries so they show up clearly on X-ray. You may feel a warm flush for a few seconds; that is normal.

    A few seconds per picture
    Clinical detail

    Low- or iso-osmolar iodinated contrast, about 6–10 mL per left coronary injection and 4–6 mL for the right, by hand or power injector; a diagnostic study typically uses 30–80 mL. Minimise volume in CKD (pre-hydration, contrast volume/eGFR ratio). The right coronary artery is filmed with a separate injection or catheter.

  6. 6Step

    X-ray pictures find the blockage

    The X-ray camera moves around you to film short movies from different angles. The doctor looks for narrowed spots. Here a blockage is found in the front artery of the heart.

    About 10 min
    Clinical detail

    Standard RAO/LAO projections with cranial or caudal tilt open up each segment (e.g., RAO cranial for mid LAD, LAO caudal "spider" for left main and LCx). A ≥70% stenosis (≥50% left main) is visually significant; intermediate lesions are tested with FFR/iFR or imaged with IVUS/OCT. The result guides medicines, PCI or bypass surgery (SYNTAX score in multivessel disease).

  7. 7Step

    Removing the catheter and sealing the artery

    The catheter and sheath are taken out. A tight wristband (or firm pressure on the groin) stops any bleeding while the tiny hole in the artery seals by itself.

    Band for about 2 hours
    Clinical detail

    Radial: compression band with "patent haemostasis" (just enough pressure to stop bleeding while keeping the artery open), deflated stepwise over ~2 h; check pulse and hand perfusion. Femoral: manual compression 15–20 min or a closure device, then bed rest 2–4 h; watch for haematoma, pseudoaneurysm and signs of retroperitoneal bleeding.

Treatment · about 1–2 hours

Angioplasty and stent (PCI), step by step

If the angiogram finds a serious narrowing, it can often be opened straight away through the same small tube: a tiny balloon widens the artery and a stent keeps it open.

  1. 1Step

    Blockage found on the angiogram

    The angiogram shows a narrowed artery that is limiting blood flow to the heart muscle. The team decides that opening it with a balloon and stent is the best choice.

    About 5 min
    Clinical detail

    Indications: acute coronary syndromes (primary PCI in STEMI, early invasive strategy in NSTE-ACS) or chronic coronary syndrome with symptoms despite medical therapy or large ischaemia. Intermediate lesions: FFR ≤0.80 or iFR ≤0.89. A 6 Fr guiding catheter (e.g., EBU/XB for the LAD) replaces the diagnostic catheter; heparin 70–100 IU/kg, target ACT ~250–300 s.

  2. 2Step

    A fine wire crosses the narrowing

    A very fine, soft wire (about a third of a millimetre thick) is steered through the narrow part and parked beyond it. It works like a rail for the next tools.

    About 2–5 min
    Clinical detail

    0.014″ workhorse wire with a shaped soft tip (hydrophilic or polymer-jacketed for tortuous or tight lesions), torqued across the lesion into the distal vessel. Confirm a distal, intraluminal position in two orthogonal views. Chronic total occlusions need dedicated wires and techniques.

  3. 3Step

    The balloon slides over the wire

    A tiny folded balloon on a thin tube slides along the wire until it sits right inside the narrowing. Small metal markers on the balloon show its exact position on X-ray.

    About 1–2 min
    Clinical detail

    Rapid-exchange (monorail) semi-compliant balloon for pre-dilatation, usually 0.8–1.0 : 1 to the reference diameter, with platinum radio-opaque markers. Heavily calcified plaque may need non-compliant, cutting/scoring balloons, intravascular lithotripsy or rotational atherectomy first.

  4. 4Step

    The balloon is inflated

    The balloon is blown up for a few seconds. It squashes the plaque against the artery wall and widens the channel. You may feel brief chest pressure; tell the team, it passes quickly.

    10–30 seconds each time
    Clinical detail

    Inflated with a ~50:50 contrast–saline mix through an indeflator to nominal pressure (typically 8–14 atm) for 10–30 s. Transient ischaemia (angina, ST changes) during occlusion is expected. Check for dissection and recoil before stenting.

  5. 5Step

    The stent is opened and locked in place

    Next a stent, a tiny metal mesh tube folded on a balloon, is placed across the narrowing. The balloon inflates, opening the mesh and pressing it firmly into the artery wall.

    About 1–2 min
    Clinical detail

    A drug-eluting stent (thin-strut cobalt- or platinum-chromium with everolimus or zotarolimus) is sized to the distal reference and long enough to land in healthy segments. Deploy at nominal or higher pressure, then post-dilate with a non-compliant balloon. IVUS/OCT guidance improves expansion and apposition and lowers adverse events.

  6. 6Step

    The balloon comes out, the stent stays

    The balloon is let down and pulled out. The stent stays in the artery for good, holding it open like scaffolding. Over the next weeks the artery lining grows over it.

    About 1 min
    Clinical detail

    The stent is permanent; endothelial coverage occurs over weeks to months (slower with DES, hence dual antiplatelet therapy). Look for edge dissection, no-reflow, thrombus or side-branch compromise; intracoronary imaging confirms minimal stent area and apposition.

  7. 7Step

    Final pictures: blood flows freely

    More dye is injected to check the result. The artery is wide open and blood rushes through to the heart muscle again. The wire and tubes are removed and the wrist or groin is sealed.

    About 5 min
    Clinical detail

    Aim for <10–20% residual stenosis, TIMI 3 flow and no dissection. Remove the wire, then the sheath, with haemostasis as after angiography. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel, ticagrelor or prasugrel) and a high-intensity statin are continued; duration depends on the clinical setting and bleeding risk.

Heart attack · emergency angioplasty

Removing a clot: thrombus aspiration

In a heart attack the artery is usually blocked by a blood clot sitting on a cracked plaque. When there is a lot of clot, the doctor can suck it out through a thin tube before placing the stent.

  1. 1Step

    A clot blocks the artery

    A plaque cracks and a blood clot quickly builds up on it until no blood can get past. The heart muscle beyond starts to suffer, so the artery must be opened as fast as possible.

    Emergency: every minute counts
    Clinical detail

    STEMI with TIMI 0 flow, most often from plaque rupture, otherwise erosion or a calcified nodule. Primary PCI within 120 min of diagnosis (wire crossing ideally ≤60–90 min). Aspirin, a potent P2Y12 inhibitor and unfractionated heparin before or during the procedure.

  2. 2Step

    A fine wire crosses the clot

    The soft wire is pushed gently through the clot and parked beyond it. This often lets a little blood trickle through again.

    About 1–2 min
    Clinical detail

    Workhorse 0.014″ wire; crossing frequently restores TIMI 1–2 flow and reveals the thrombus burden. TIMI thrombus grade 4–5 (large thrombus, ≥2 vessel diameters, or occlusion) is where aspiration is most often considered.

  3. 3Step

    The aspiration catheter reaches the clot

    A thin tube with an open tip slides over the wire up to the clot. Its other end is joined to a large syringe that can create strong suction.

    About 1 min
    Clinical detail

    Manual rapid-exchange aspiration catheters (6 Fr guide compatible, e.g., Export, Pronto) with a 30–40 mL locking syringe, or mechanical continuous aspiration (e.g., Penumbra CAT RX). If the catheter will not cross, a small balloon predilatation can help.

  4. 4Step

    Suction pulls the clot out

    With suction on, the tube is moved slowly through the clot. Pieces of clot are pulled into the tip and out of the body into the syringe. This may be repeated a few times.

    A few passes, about 1–3 min
    Clinical detail

    Start suction before entering the thrombus and advance slowly proximal to distal; keep suction on while withdrawing into the guide, then back-bleed the guide to clear thrombus and air (risk of stroke from embolised clot). Routine aspiration in STEMI is not recommended (TASTE, TOTAL: no mortality benefit; more strokes in TOTAL); it may be used selectively or as bailout for a large residual thrombus.

  5. 5Step

    Blood flows again

    With the clot gone, blood rushes back into the heart muscle. The removed clot can be seen in a small filter. The cracked plaque underneath is still narrowed.

    Checked straight away
    Clinical detail

    Assess TIMI flow and myocardial blush. Treat no-reflow with intracoronary adenosine, verapamil, nitroprusside or epinephrine; consider a GP IIb/IIIa inhibitor for large residual thrombus. Inspect the aspirate: red (fibrin/RBC) versus white (platelet-rich) thrombus.

  6. 6Step

    A stent seals the cracked plaque

    A stent is opened across the cracked plaque so it cannot set off a new clot, and the artery stays wide open. Blood-thinning tablets are then taken every day.

    About 5–10 min
    Clinical detail

    Stent the culprit with a DES, sized by imaging where possible (thrombus and vasoconstriction cause undersizing). Routine deferred stenting is not recommended (DANAMI-3-DEFER). After ACS: DAPT usually for 12 months (ticagrelor or prasugrel preferred), high-intensity statin and cardiac rehabilitation.

After the procedure

Risks and recovery

Most people recover quickly. This is a typical path; follow the plan your own team gives you.

  1. Day 0

    Procedure day

    • Wristband or groin pressure for a few hours
    • Drink plenty of water to flush out the dye
    • Rest the arm or leg; nurses check the site and pulse
  2. Day 1

    First day home

    • Light walking around the house
    • Keep the dressing clean and dry; a small bruise is normal
    • Do not drive until your doctor says you can
  3. Week 1

    Getting back to normal

    • No heavy lifting (over 4–5 kg) with that arm or straining
    • Most people return to desk work
    • Take every medicine daily; never stop antiplatelets on your own
  4. Month 1

    Building strength

    • Follow-up visit with your cardiologist
    • Cardiac rehab and a gradual exercise plan
    • Heart-healthy food, no smoking, control sugar and blood pressure

When to call your doctor

Chest pain

Pain, pressure or tightness in the chest, especially at rest. Call an ambulance.

Bleeding at the site

Press firmly on the spot with your fingers for 10 minutes and get help.

Swelling or a hard lump

Growing swelling, or a hand or leg that turns cold, pale, numb or painful.

Fever or redness

Fever, spreading redness, warmth or pus around the puncture site.

Frequently asked questions

Most people feel only the sting of the numbing injection and some pressure at the wrist or groin. You cannot feel the catheter inside the arteries. The dye may cause a brief warm flush, and during balloon inflation you may feel chest pressure for a few seconds.

An angiogram takes about 20–45 minutes on the table; an angioplasty usually 30 minutes to 2 hours. With preparation and recovery, plan for most of the day in hospital.

Often yes, especially after a planned angiogram through the wrist. After angioplasty many people go home the same evening or the next morning. After a heart attack the stay is usually a few days. Someone should drive you home.

A stent is permanent and does not wear out or move. The artery lining grows over it within weeks to months. A small number of stents narrow again (re-stenosis), and new plaque can form in other arteries, so medicines and a healthy lifestyle matter for life.

Never stop them early unless your cardiologist tells you, even before dental work or surgery: ask first. A statin and other heart medicines are often added.
