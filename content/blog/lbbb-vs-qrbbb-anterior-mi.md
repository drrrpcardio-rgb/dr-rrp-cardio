## Introduction

Two patients with an acute anterior MI reached our cath lab in the same week. Both had a complete occlusion of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), and both had a bundle branch block on the ECG. One ECG hid the infarct almost completely. The other practically drew a map to the blockage.

The difference was the type of block: **left bundle branch block (LBBB)** in the first patient, **qRBBB** in the second.

## Why the type of block matters

- **LBBB changes the whole QRS.** The septum activates from right to left, the left ventricle depolarises late and slowly, and the ST segments are abnormal before any infarct begins. ST elevation in V1–V3 is *expected*: the sign we look for in a STEMI is part of the block's own pattern.
- **RBBB changes only the end of the QRS.** The first 40–60 ms, which reflects septal and left ventricular activation, is preserved. Q waves and ST elevation can still be read in the usual way.
- **qRBBB is RBBB with a qR in V1** instead of the usual rsR′. The small initial r wave, produced by septal activation, has been lost. In a patient with chest pain and anterior ST elevation, that missing r wave is a marker of septal necrosis: RBBB *plus* an anteroseptal infarct. (A qR in V1 has other causes too, such as RV hypertrophy and acute RV strain, so always read it in context.)

![Schematic of lead V1 in normal conduction (rS), RBBB (rsR′), qRBBB with acute MI (qR with ST elevation) and LBBB (QS with discordant ST elevation)](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/v1-morphology.svg)

*Figure 1. Lead V1 compared. The small initial r wave is septal activation. RBBB keeps it; in qRBBB it is replaced by a q wave; in LBBB the whole complex is negative and ST elevation is expected.*

In short: **LBBB hides an infarct; qRBBB announces one.**

## Case 1: the infarct hidden behind LBBB

A 70-year-old woman with diabetes and hypertension of 15 years' duration presented late in the evening. She had no chest pain. She had a day of intermittent **back pain** that finally stopped settling and began radiating to the axilla. Blood pressure was 170/100 mmHg and random blood glucose 238 mg/dL.

The ECG showed LBBB with left axis deviation. There were deep QS complexes in V1–V3 with ST elevation, but discordant to the QRS, exactly as LBBB produces on its own. Nothing on the tracing declared an acute infarct.

![12-lead ECG of case 1 showing left bundle branch block with left axis deviation](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/case1-lbbb-ecg.jpg)

*Figure 2. Case 1: LBBB with left axis deviation (QRS 134 ms). The ST changes in V1–V3 are discordant, as expected for the block.*

> In LBBB, the ECG can stay silent over a completely occluded artery. Listen to the patient.
>
> — Case 1 lesson

What raised suspicion was the patient, not the paper. In an elderly diabetic woman, persistent unexplained back and axillary pain is an acute coronary syndrome until proven otherwise. A bedside echo settled it: the distal septum and apex were severely hypokinetic, LVEF was 30%, and there was an LV thrombus.

## Case 2: the infarct that qRBBB gave away

A 61-year-old man had a classic presentation: crushing left-sided chest pain from 9:30 a.m., with vomiting and profuse sweating. An angiogram earlier in the year had shown triple-vessel disease.

The ECG was unambiguous: RBBB with a **qR in V1**, marked left axis deviation (left anterior fascicular block), and **ST elevation in V2–V6, I and aVL**.

![12-lead ECG of case 2 showing RBBB pattern, left anterior fascicular block and anterolateral ST elevation](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/case2-qrbbb-ecg-a.jpg)

*Figure 5. Case 2: left anterior fascicular block and anterolateral ST elevation. On this tracing the QRS measured 92 ms, an incomplete RBBB pattern.*

![Second 12-lead ECG of case 2 showing qRBBB, left anterior fascicular block and ST elevation in V2 to V6, I and aVL](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/case2-qrbbb-ecg-b.jpg)

*Figure 6. Case 2: complete RBBB (QRS 129 ms) with a qR in V1, left anterior fascicular block and extensive anterolateral ST elevation (V2–V6, I, aVL).*

The QRS width differed between the two tracings: 92 ms on one, 129 ms on the other. Conduction in the septum was unstable, which is itself a warning sign in a proximal LAD occlusion.

> The ECG said proximal LAD before the angiogram did.
>
> — Case 2 lesson

Every feature pointed to the same place:

- **qR in V1:** the septum is already infarcting.
- **RBBB + LAFB:** the right bundle and the left anterior fascicle both run through the anterior septum, supplied by the first septal perforators of the LAD. Losing both places the occlusion *proximal* to those branches.
- **ST elevation in V2–V6, I and aVL:** a very large territory at risk.

![Schematic of the ventricular conduction system: the right bundle branch and left anterior fascicle run through the upper septum, supplied by the first septal perforators of the LAD; the left posterior fascicle has a dual supply](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/conduction-system-lad-supply.svg)

*Figure 7. The right bundle branch and the left anterior fascicle share the upper septum and its blood supply from the first septal perforators. An occlusion above those branches knocks out both, producing RBBB + LAFB.*

The ECG said *proximal LAD*, and angiography confirmed it: a **100% thrombus-laden occlusion at the ostium of the LAD**. The lesion was crossed with a wire, thrombus was aspirated, and a 3.5 × 24 mm drug-eluting stent was deployed, followed by high-pressure post-dilatation.

## Reading through LBBB: the Sgarbossa and Smith criteria

When LBBB clouds the picture, look for ST changes that break the block's own rules:

1. **Concordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm** in any lead with a positive QRS.
2. **Concordant ST depression ≥ 1 mm in V1–V3.**
3. **Excessively discordant ST elevation:** ST elevation ≥ 1 mm that is at least 25% of the depth of the preceding S wave. This is the Smith modification, which replaced Sgarbossa's original fixed cut-off of ≥ 5 mm.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> In the derivation study it was about 91% sensitive and 90% specific for an acutely occluded artery.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>

![Schematic of the Sgarbossa and Smith criteria: expected proportionate discordant ST elevation in LBBB, concordant ST elevation, concordant ST depression in V1 to V3, and excessively discordant ST elevation with an ST/S ratio of at least 25%](/images/blog/lbbb-vs-qrbbb/sgarbossa-smith-criteria.svg)

*Figure 11. What is expected in LBBB (left) and the three patterns that suggest an acute coronary occlusion.*

Any one of these warrants activation of the cath lab. A negative result does not exclude MI in LBBB, as our first patient showed. Try them on her ECG in Figure 2: check each lead for concordance, then compare the ST elevation in V1–V3 with the depth of the S wave before it.

The 2023 ESC guidelines are clear: in a patient with suspected ongoing ischaemia, LBBB or RBBB should prompt a primary PCI strategy, as for STEMI, **whether or not the block was known to be old**.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a>,<a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>

## Six take-home points

1. **LBBB hides; qRBBB reveals.**
2. In LBBB, when the ECG is quiet, trust the symptoms and the echo.
3. In a patient with chest pain, a **qR in V1** means septal infarction: look for anterior ST elevation.
4. **RBBB + LAFB + anterior ST elevation = proximal LAD** until proven otherwise. Watch for progression to complete heart block.
5. Older patients, women and people with diabetes often present with back, jaw or arm pain rather than chest pain. Think of the heart first.
6. **Image the apex after every anterior MI.** Both patients had an LV thrombus, a reason to change their antithrombotic treatment and follow them up with imaging.

## Test yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why can ST elevation in V1–V3 be normal in LBBB but not in RBBB?</summary>

In LBBB the whole sequence of ventricular activation is abnormal, so repolarisation is too: ST segments move opposite (discordant) to the main QRS deflection, and V1–V3 are mostly negative. In RBBB the early QRS and the ST segment are largely unaffected, so ST elevation there means what it usually means.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What does the "q" in qRBBB tell you?</summary>

The small initial r wave in V1 comes from septal activation. When it is replaced by a q wave, septal myocardium has been lost. With chest pain and anterior ST elevation, that means an anteroseptal infarct.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. RBBB, left anterior fascicular block and ST elevation in V2–V6, I and aVL: where is the culprit?</summary>

The proximal LAD, above the first septal and diagonal branches. The right bundle and the left anterior fascicle both depend on the first septal perforators, and the lateral leads point to loss of the first diagonal.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. In LBBB, the ST elevation in V2 is 4 mm and the S wave before it is 12 mm deep. Is the Smith criterion met?</summary>

Yes. The ST elevation is at least 1 mm and the ratio is 4 / 12 ≈ 33%, above the 25% threshold. That is excessively discordant ST elevation, which suggests an acute coronary occlusion.

</details>
